\section{Introduction}
Predicting the move a human will play, rather than the best move, underpins human-like engines,
tutoring and analysis tools. The Maia line of work~\cite{maia1,maia2} and ALLIE~\cite{allie}
established the task; Chessformer~\cite{chessformer} (released as \textsc{Maia-3}) is the current
open state of the art at 57.1\% move-matching accuracy with 79M parameters.

We started by reproducing Maia-3: we downloaded all nine released checkpoints, rebuilt the exact
884,049-position evaluation protocol, and benchmarked every variant (top-1, top-3, cross-entropy).
The numbers reproduce, but reading the paper next to the released code exposed a series of
inaccuracies and design decisions that leave accuracy and efficiency on the table
(Section~\ref{sec:audit}). Three matter most for a human-move model:
(i) the network never sees the \emph{clock}, and the protocol discards every position played under
time pressure; (ii) history is eight board snapshots that alternate orientation every ply and are
fused by a single linear map, which is why ``more history does not help''; (iii) skill is encoded by
concatenating two 128-d interpolated vectors to every one of the 64 tokens, i.e.\ two scalars
occupying 73\% of the input.

\textbf{Cloverb} keeps what works in Chessformer (squares as tokens, a from--to policy head) and
re-designs the rest around the information a human player actually has and around efficiency:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Inputs a player sees.} The player's own Elo (not the opponent's), the time control
(base seconds and increment), the time left on the player's clock, and the last $\le 64$ plies as
\emph{moves} scatter-added onto the squares they touched, all in one mover-relative frame; castling
rights live on the rook squares and the en-passant square is marked.
\item \textbf{Conditioning where it acts.} Elo and clock features form one vector that modulates
every block through adaptive normalisation (adaLN-Zero~\cite{dit}) instead of being copied 64 times.
\item \textbf{Geometry as a prior, not a generator.} Every head of every layer owns a static
$64\times64$ attention bias, \emph{initialised} with chess movement patterns (rook lines,
diagonals, knight jumps, king steps, pawn captures). This one change makes Cloverb learn
5.6 points faster than the Maia-3 architecture at equal data; Chessformer's dynamic bias
generator turns out to be unnecessary.
\item \textbf{Systems.} A C chess core that parses Lichess PGN at $>$0.3M plies/s and builds training
batches by replaying games on the fly (3.6\,$\mu$s/sample, 6 bytes of storage per ply); a fused
AVX-512 attention kernel for 64-token sequences; bf16 training on Intel AMX with
\texttt{torch.compile}; jemalloc. Everything in this paper ran on \emph{one 4-core CPU}.
\end{itemize}

\section{Background}
\textbf{Maia}~\cite{maia1} trained one CNN per rating band; \textbf{Maia-2}~\cite{maia2} unified them
with skill-aware attention over CNN channels; \textbf{ALLIE}~\cite{allie} is a decoder-only
transformer over move sequences with clock and Elo tokens; \textbf{Chessformer / Maia-3}~\cite{chessformer}
uses an encoder over 64 square tokens with Geometric Attention Bias (GAB), an attention bias
generated from a compressed board representation mixing shared templates. \textbf{Allie 2.0}~\cite{allie2},
a 5.6B-parameter mixture-of-experts trained on 75B tokens with eight GPUs, reports a 0.31-point
top-1 gain over Maia-3-79M on Maia's protocol and shows that the clock matters most in time trouble.

\paragraph{Protocol.} The ALLIE test set~\cite{allie} contains 18,239 Lichess blitz games from 2022.
Dropping each game's first 10 plies and every ply from the first one whose mover has fewer than 30\,s
selects \emph{exactly} the 884,049 positions reported in the paper. We score a fixed sample of
100,016 positions (2,101 whole games) of that protocol with the official model code (bf16 trunk,
fp32 policy head; Table~\ref{tab:maia3}). Our sample is about 0.25 points harder than the full set
for every variant; all orderings and gaps of the paper's ablations are reproduced.

\paragraph{Findings.} The full list with evidence is in the repository
(\texttt{docs/critique.md}); the most consequential are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{The released engine runs without history by default.} \texttt{maia3-uci} fills all
history slots with the current position unless \texttt{--use-uci-history} is passed; this costs
\input{tables/nohist_cost} points of top-1 accuracy, more than training without history at all.
\item \emph{History is alternating and input-only.} Each past board is mirrored to its own side to
move, so consecutive frames are flipped relative to each other, and all frames are fused by one
linear map. The paper's conclusion that 32 positions are no better than 8 is a property of this
encoding (we reproduce it: \textsc{hist32} vs.\ 5M), not of chess.
\item \emph{No clock, and time pressure is removed from training and evaluation.} The unreleased-in-code
\textsc{Maia3-23M-ponder} checkpoint, which does read the clock, is better even on the
pressure-free protocol (Table~\ref{tab:maia3}).
\item \emph{Elo as concatenated soft embeddings} is affine in the rating (the paper concedes it is
equivalent to a scalar) yet fills 256 of 352 input channels on all 64 tokens.
\item \emph{Missing state}: castling rights, en passant and the 50-move counter are absent from the
human models although the paper lists them as necessary.
\item \emph{Accounting}: ``FLOPs'' are multiply--accumulates (2$\times$ off); parameter counts do not
match the checkpoints; an undocumented third (``ponder'') head exists and one released checkpoint
cannot be loaded by the released code; the value head is $d$ wide, not 128; ``all code and data''
is open-sourced but no training code or data is.
\item \emph{Missing baseline}: GAB is compared to absolute embeddings and to a translation-invariant
$15\times15$ relative bias, but not to a static, per-square $64\times64$ bias, so the benefit of GAB
being \emph{dynamic} is not isolated (the paper's own Table~3 reports 0.77 cross-position correlation
of GAB maps).
\item \emph{Evaluation precision}: on CPUs, bf16 policy logits tie often enough that a strict rank
count inflates top-1 by about one point (55.3\%$\to$56.4\% for Maia-3-5M); we compute all policy heads
in fp32.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Cloverb}
\subsection{Inputs}
All squares are expressed from the mover's point of view (vertical flip and colour swap when Black
moves). Token $s$ starts as
\[
x_s = E_{\text{piece}}[p_s] + E_{\text{sq}}[s]
 + \textstyle\sum_{r} \mathbf{1}[\text{right } r]\,\mathbf{1}[s = \text{rook}(r)]\,E_{\text{castle}}[r]
 + \mathbf{1}[s=\text{ep}]\,E_{\text{ep}}
 + \sum_{j=1}^{64} \big(\mathbf{1}[s=f_j] F_j + \mathbf{1}[s=t_j] T_j\big),
\]
where $(f_j,t_j)$ are the from/to squares of the move played $j$ plies ago and
$F_j = E_{\text{from}}[j] + E_{\text{pc}}^{\text{from}}[\text{side}_j,\text{piece}_j]$,
$T_j = E_{\text{to}}[j] + E_{\text{pc}}^{\text{to}}[\text{side}_j,\text{piece}_j] + \mathbf{1}[\text{capture}_j]E_{\text{cap}}[\text{side}_j]$.
The history term is a scatter-add of 128 vectors per position: it costs $O(64d)$ instead of a
$(12n{+}256)\times d$ projection per token, it is lossless about the move sequence, and it lets the
\emph{same} square token accumulate everything that happened on it.

The conditioning vector $c=\mathrm{MLP}(\phi)$ is built from: a hat-function (linear spline)
encoding of the player's Elo on 15 anchors (400--3200), a hat encoding of $\log(1+\text{time left})$
on 14 anchors (2\,s--1\,h), $\log$ base, $\log$ increment, time left relative to
$\text{base}+40\,\text{inc}$, the halfmove and fullmove counters, castling rights, check, and the
number of available history plies. $c$ is added to every token and drives adaLN-Zero modulation
(scale, shift and residual gate for attention and MLP) in every block.

\subsection{Trunk and heads}
Each block applies attention with RMS-normalised queries and keys~\cite{qknorm} and an additive
bias $B_{\ell h} = S_{\ell h} + \sum_{t=1}^{T} a_{\ell h t}(\bar x, c)\, U_t$: $S_{\ell h}\in\mathbb{R}^{64\times64}$
is a static learned bias per layer and head, and the optional second term mixes $T$ templates shared
by all layers with coefficients from the mean token and $c$ (a cheap GAB analogue). The static
biases are initialised with chess geometry: head 1 gets $+2$ on rook lines, head 2 on diagonals,
head 3 on knight jumps, head 4 on king steps, head 5 on pawn-capture squares, head 6 on the same
file, head 7 a $-0.5\cdot$Chebyshev-distance locality term, head 8 starts at zero
(Section~\ref{sec:diag} shows that this prior is what matters and that $T{=}0$ suffices).
The MLP is $W_2\,\mathrm{ReLU}(W_1 h)^2$~\cite{relu2}. Blocks are post-norm, as in Chessformer:
$x \leftarrow \mathrm{RMSNorm}(x + (1+g_1)\,\mathrm{Attn}(x(1+s_1)+b_1))$ and likewise for the MLP,
with $(s,b,g)$ from adaLN-Zero; output projections are zero-initialised. The move history also
records which piece was captured.

The policy head scores move $(u\!\to\!v)$ as $\langle W_f x_u, W_t x_v\rangle/\sqrt d$ over Maia-3's
4352-move vocabulary (64$\times$64 plus 256 promotions, whose piece bias comes from the destination
token), so Maia-3 checkpoints can serve as teachers. Two auxiliary heads read
$[\bar x, c]$: game outcome (WDL) and think time (32 log-spaced buckets), the latter giving the
engine a human-like time-management signal. The training loss is
$\mathcal{L} = \mathrm{CE}_{\text{legal}}(\text{move}) + 0.1\,\mathrm{CE}(\text{WDL}) + 0.05\,\mathrm{CE}(\text{time})$,
with the softmax restricted to legal moves.

\subsection{Systems}
\paragraph{Data.} Games are stored whole: per ply a 16-bit move, the 16-bit clock before the move and
the 16-bit time spent; per game ratings, time control and result. A C core parses Lichess SAN
movetext with clock comments (validated move-for-move and clock-for-clock against
\texttt{python-chess}), and builds batches by replaying each sampled game to the sampled ply,
emitting the board, castling/en-passant state, the 64-ply history and a legal-move bitmask in
$\approx$3.6\,$\mu$s per sample on one core, in a background thread (ctypes releases the GIL).
5.5M games (372M plies) occupy 2.2\,GB and any position can be sampled in any epoch.

\paragraph{Compute.} Training uses bf16 autocast on Intel AMX with \texttt{torch.compile}. Profiling
showed that GEMMs were only a third of a step: attention over $B\cdot H$ tiny $64\times64$ problems
and memory-bound pointwise kernels dominated. PyTorch's fused CPU attention reached only
$\approx$26\,GFLOP/s on this shape, so we wrote a fused AVX-512 kernel that keeps each head's
$64\times64$ scores in L1, fuses the static and template biases, and recomputes scores in the
backward pass (custom \texttt{torch.library} op, exact to $2\cdot10^{-6}$ against PyTorch in fp32).
Replacing glibc malloc by jemalloc removed page-fault storms on large tensors (+30\%).
