\subsection{Equal-data comparison with Chessformer}
\input{tables/arch}
We trained Cloverb (d=256, 8 layers, MLP$\times$2, FLOP-matched to Maia-3-5M: 0.30 vs.\ 0.32
GMAC per position) and the authors' own Chessformer-5M module (inputs as in \texttt{maia3.uci} with
\texttt{--use-uci-history}: the last 8 boards and both Elos) on the identical stream of 1.54M positions with the same optimiser, schedule
shape and evaluation (Table~\ref{tab:arch}). Two findings shaped the final system.

\emph{First, Chessformer's recipe is the bottleneck, not its architecture.} With a sensible
learning rate, pre-processing and kernels, the published 5M architecture learns fast:
45.9\% after 1.5M positions, i.e.\ 81\% of the way from chance (a uniform guess among legal moves,
6.1\%) to its published accuracy after 0.3\% of its published training data. The first Cloverb configuration learned \emph{more slowly
per position} (44.7\%) even though it trains about 2\% faster per second; Section~\ref{sec:diag}
isolates why.

\emph{Second, a teacher is worth more than data.} Distilling~\cite{hinton} the released Maia-3-23M online
(soft targets with weight 0.7 plus the human move) lifts Cloverb from 37.5\% to 42.9\% at 0.5M
positions, beyond what the student reaches with twice the data. Because the teacher's forward
pass costs about as much as a student training step, we amortise it: the final model trains for
several epochs on a fixed pool of positions labelled once by the clock-aware
Maia-3-23M-ponder checkpoint (top-16 moves, which hold 99.2\% of the probability mass).

\subsection{Diagnosing the per-sample gap: geometry is the missing prior}\label{sec:diag}
\input{tables/diag}
We ran single-factor diagnostics for 512k positions each on the identical data stream
(Table~\ref{tab:diag}). One change dominates everything else: \emph{initialising the static
attention biases with chess geometry}. Each of the first heads of every layer starts with a
$+2$ logit bonus on the squares reachable by one movement pattern (rook lines, bishop diagonals,
knight jumps, king steps, pawn-capture squares, same file) and one head with a Chebyshev-distance
penalty; the biases remain fully trainable. This single prior lifts Cloverb from 37.5\% to
44.4\% after 512k positions, \emph{5.5 points above the Maia-3 architecture} at equal data (44.37\%
vs.\ 38.84\%; that run used Chessformer's lower peak learning rate, $5\times10^{-4}$), and nearly matches
(44.4\% vs.\ 44.7\%) what the zero-initialised model reached with three times the data. It bears on
the paper's missing baseline: early in training, much of what a dynamic bias could contribute is
geometry, which a static, per-square bias with the right initialisation supplies at a fraction of the
cost (we did not retrain Chessformer itself with such a bias).
Post-norm blocks (as in Chessformer) add a further 0.6 points on top of the geometric prior;
encoding \emph{which} piece was captured in the move history adds 0.3 points for one extra
embedding lookup; and the dynamic template mix contributes nothing (44.35\% without vs.\ 44.37\% with, equal
cross-entropy), so the final model drops it and keeps only static, geometry-initialised biases.

\subsection{Inference}
\input{tables/infer}
Cloverb ships as a standalone C program (one translation unit, two source files) with no Python or
PyTorch dependency at run time: the C chess core encodes the
position and its history, the trunk's GEMMs run on AMX bf16 tiles with weights packed once into VNNI
tiles, attention runs in AVX-512 with each head's scores kept in registers and L1, and the policy head
stays in fp32. On 500 random positions of the fresh 2026 games, an fp32 build (AVX-512, no AMX)
reproduces the PyTorch forward pass of the trained weights to within $2.2\cdot10^{-6}$ in every
legal-move probability and the AMX bf16 build to within $2.6\cdot10^{-3}$ (mean KL $2.4\cdot10^{-6}$
nats), both with the same top move on every position (\texttt{results/engine\_check.json}).

\section{Limitations}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Compute.} Every number here comes from 4-core Xeon CPUs, one at a time, over a few days
(the container moved between hosts: hosts with AMX ran most of training, the Maia-3 benchmarks and
the speed measurements; a host without AMX ran the last 5.5k training steps and the final Cloverb
evaluation, both in fp32). Cloverb saw
tens of millions of positions, Maia-3 half a billion. Our conclusions about architecture are about
learning speed in this regime; at Maia-3's budget the geometric prior's advantage may shrink.
\item \textbf{Teacher.} The final model is distilled from a released Maia-3 checkpoint (with the
human move as a second target), so its accuracy partly inherits Maia-3's; the equal-data
comparisons in Table~\ref{tab:diag} and the first two rows of Table~\ref{tab:arch} use no teacher. Maia-3 never trained on
positions with less than 30\,s on the clock, so in time trouble the human move keeps a weight of 0.6
in our loss (elsewhere 0.3 for the first 24k steps and 0 afterwards).
\item \textbf{Opponent rating.} By design Cloverb sees only the mover's rating, as an engine user
would set it; Maia-3 also sees the opponent's.
\item \textbf{Test sets.} The ALLIE test set is blitz from 2022; our training games are from 2025.
The fresh 2026 test set covers all time controls, but only 615 of its 120k games are classical
(390 of them are scored), and each all-plies set comes from fewer than 400 games.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
Reproducing Maia-3 confirmed its accuracy and exposed avoidable losses in its inputs, defaults and
evaluation. Cloverb reorganises the same square-token idea around what a human sees at the board
(own rating, clock, the moves that led here) and around the hardware it runs on, and shows that a
simple geometric prior on attention, not a dynamic bias generator, is what lets such a model learn
chess quickly.
